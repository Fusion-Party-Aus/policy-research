## 1. Reviewer Panel Appraisals

### Reviewer 1 (Journal-Fit / Housing Policy Specialist)
- **Assessment:** The research brief successfully articulates the policy tension between upstream private rental sustainment and long-term capital social housing provision. The focus on acute eviction prevention fills a major gap in the Victorian policy debate.
- **Strengths:** Excellent integration of Victorian statutory mechanisms (RTA ss 330A, 91ZM, 91V) with empirical literature. Clear delineation of unit costs ($1.5k–$2.5k) versus downstream crisis expenditure ($15k–$30k).
- **Critical Challenge:** The brief must avoid presenting private rental sustainment as a permanent solution. Private rental brokerage is a triage mechanism. In an unfettered market with sub-1.5% vacancy, private tenancies remain inherently insecure. The policy narrative must firmly anchor Option 4 (social housing supply) as the only durable solution for households with permanent zero-to-low earning capacity.

### Reviewer 2 (Causal Methods & Econometrics Reviewer)
- **Assessment:** The review draws appropriately on high-quality causal designs (Evans et al. 2016 Science quasi-experiment; Greiner et al. 2013 Harvard Law Review RCT; Collinson & Reed 2023 QJE instrumental variable study).
- **Critical Challenge (The Attrition and LATE Caveats):**
  1. *Local Average Treatment Effects (LATE):* The Evans et al. (2016) and Collinson & Reed (2023) estimates represent marginal treatment effects specific to court-involved or call-center-connecting populations. They cannot be generalized uncritically to all renters in informal arrears.
  2. *Follow-Up Attrition:* Victorian program evaluations (such as Watson et al. 2020 on PRAP) exhibit significant loss-to-follow-up at 12 months (~15–20%). If non-respondents experienced eviction and housing loss at higher rates, the reported 80% sustainment rate suffers from positive survivor bias. The brief should explicitly bound this uncertainty.
  3. *Distinguishing Association from Causation in Administrative Data:* AIHW SHS data reports a 91% housing maintenance rate during closed support, but lacks an untreated control group. Clients assisted by SHS may possess greater motivation or informal networks than unassisted individuals who sleep rough.

### Reviewer 3 (Equity, First Nations & Family Violence Reviewer)
- **Assessment:** The brief rightly highlights the profound overrepresentation of Aboriginal Victorians (17.1% of SHS caseload) and family violence survivors (42.8% of female clients).
- **Critical Challenge (The Coercion and Self-Determination Risk):**
  1. *Aboriginal Self-Determination:*Mainstream tenancy sustainment programs administered by non-Indigenous community agencies often impose paternalistic budgeting requirements that conflict with cultural obligations (kinship sharing, caring for extended family). Tenancy sustainment for First Nations renters must be transferred to Aboriginal Community Controlled Housing Organisations (ACCHOs).
  2. *Survivor Autonomy:* Tenancy sustainment must not become an ideological goal that overrides safety. If staying in a home exposes a woman and children to perpetrator stalking or trauma, "sustaining the tenancy" is a harmful policy failure. The brief must emphasize that facilitating a rapid, safe exit to a new home is an equally valid prevention outcome.

### Reviewer 4 (Devil's Advocate — Core Structural Challenges)
The Devil's Advocate specifically interrogates the four weakest assumptions underlying upstream eviction prevention:

#### Challenge 1: The "Efficiency Trap" / Low Targeting Specificity
- *The Objection:* Proponents claim prevention saves thousands of dollars in crisis costs. But empirical studies by Shinn et al. (2013) ([S24](sources.md#s24)) and Burt et al. (2007) ([S25](sources.md#s25)) demonstrate that **only 10% to 15% of families facing rent arrears would actually enter a homeless shelter if unassisted**. The other 85% resolve arrears through informal loans, family doubling-up, or finding cheaper housing.
- *The Implication:* For every 100 households given a $2,000 grant ($200,000 total spend), only ~15 would have become homeless. The real cost per averted homelessness episode is not $2,000, but **$13,333** ($200,000 / 15). If this deadweight loss is not controlled through strict screening for formal VCAT eviction notices, the claimed fiscal benefit-cost ratio evaporates.

#### Challenge 2: Landlord Subsidy Capture and Moral Hazard
- *The Objection:* Providing predictable state grants to clear rent arrears alters market incentives. Private landlords who know the state will bail out tenants in arrears may be less willing to negotiate rent reductions, quicker to issue notices to vacate, and more inclined to raise rents above market rates ([S12](sources.md#s12)).
- *The Implication:* State cash grants risk becoming a taxpayer subsidy to landlords who maintain substandard, unaffordable dwellings ("slumlord subsidies"), propping up unviable tenancies without improving tenant welfare.

#### Challenge 3: The 24-Month Decay Rate of One-Off Interventions
- *The Objection:* Emergency grants clear past arrears, but do not change the tenant's ongoing income or the market rent. Longitudinal studies (Taylor & Johnson 2021 [S21](sources.md#s21); Parkinson et al. 2019 [S29](sources.md#s29)) show that within 18 to 24 months, low-income renters who spend >50% of income on rent experience recurrent arrears and face eviction again.
- *The Implication:* Celebrating "80% retention at 6 months" is an exercise in artificial horizon truncation. Unless the intervention permanently lowers the rent (via social housing) or raises household income, one-off sustainment merely delays eviction rather than preventing it.

#### Challenge 4: The Cashable Savings Illusion
- *The Objection:* Economic evaluations claim prevention saves the state $15,000 to $30,000 per episode across health, justice, and emergency housing.
- *The Implication:* Hospital beds, police cars, and emergency shelter contracts operate on fixed capital and staffing overheads. Preventing 100 evictions does not enable the Treasurer to cut hospital or police budgets by $1.5 million. These savings are non-cashable congestion reductions. Presenting them as cashable offsets risks budgetary discredit.

## 2. Synthesis and Editorial Decision

| Reviewer Role | Primary Recommendation | Required Revision / Qualification in Final Pack |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Journal-Fit** | Accept with minor qualifications | Explicitly label private rental sustainment as a triage stopgap, subordinate to social housing. |
| **Causal Methods** | Accept with methodological caveats | Disclose survivor bias in 12-month cohort studies; bound the LATE applicability of US trials. |
| **Equity & Services** | Accept with mandatory First Nations caveat | Mandate ACCHO governance; ensure survivor-led exit is treated as a valid prevention success. |
| **Devil's Advocate** | Challenge accepted; refine policy boundaries | Address the Efficiency Trap by restricting grants to formal VCAT notice recipients; debunk cashable savings illusions. |

### Final Synthesis Verdict:
The evidence-led response to Victorian tenancy loss is **robust and actionable, provided it is deployed as an integrated, tightly screened triage package**. 
- One-off cash relief (PRAP) must be restricted to households facing formal statutory notices to avoid deadweight loss.
- Arrears relief must be paired with duty lawyer advocacy at VCAT to enforce statutory rights under RTA s 330A.
- Policy makers must recognize that private rental sustainment manages immediate humanitarian crisis and prevents acute downstream trauma, but cannot cure the structural disease of aggregate private market unaffordability.

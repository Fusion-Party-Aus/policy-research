## Descriptive Baseline Stratum

### S01
- **Citation:** Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW). (2024). *Specialist Homelessness Services annual report 2023–24*. Canberra: AIHW.
- **URL / Identifier:** [AIHW SHS Annual Report](https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/homelessness-services/specialist-homelessness-services-annual-report)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria & National, reporting financial year 2023–24 (published December 2024).
- **Design & Cohort:** Universal administrative collection across all funded Specialist Homelessness Services (SHS). Victorian sample: 105,400 distinct clients assisted.
- **Key Findings:** Victoria assists the highest volume of SHS clients in Australia. Approximately 60% of clients present *housed but at risk of homelessness* (due to housing crisis, rent arrears, or family violence). Among clients presenting housed at risk, 91.2% maintain housing during direct support episodes. However, post-support outcomes beyond closure are unrecorded. Family and domestic violence is the leading primary reason for seeking assistance among females (42.8%). Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander clients represent 17.1% of the Victorian caseload despite comprising ~1% of the state population.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Administrative collection captures only funded agency service users; unassisted households and hidden homelessness (e.g. severe overcrowding, couch surfing) are excluded.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Direct primary Victorian statutory data.

### S02
- **Citation:** Department of Families, Fairness and Housing (DFFH). (2025). *Victorian Housing Register (VHR) Transition Data and Allocations Report: 2024–25*. Melbourne: State of Victoria.
- **URL / Identifier:** [DFFH VHR Data](https://www.dffh.vic.gov.au/publications/victorian-housing-register-data)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria, 2024–25 financial year.
- **Design & Cohort:** Administrative data tracking all applications on the unified public/community housing waitlist. Priority access cohort includes people experiencing homelessness, family violence, and severe housing breakdown.
- **Key Findings:** Total applicants on the VHR exceed 55,000 households, with over 30,000 on the Priority Access category. Mean waiting time for priority access allocations reached 17.2 months in 2024–25 (up from 16.5 months in 2023–24). General waitlist applications face multi-year or indeterminate queues.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Registry measures rationing of scarce state dwellings rather than total latent housing need.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Authoritative official Victorian administrative baseline.

### S03
- **Citation:** Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (VCAT). (2024). *VCAT Annual Report 2023–24: Residential Tenancies List*. Melbourne: VCAT.
- **URL / Identifier:** [VCAT Annual Report 2023–24](https://www.vcat.vic.gov.au/about-vcat/annual-reports-and-strategic-directions)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria, reporting 2023–24 tribunal year.
- **Design & Cohort:** Statutory tribunal registry recording all residential tenancy dispute applications, hearings, and orders across metropolitan and regional Victoria (48,210 applications).
- **Key Findings:** The Residential Tenancies List represents ~58% of all VCAT matters statewide. Applications for possession orders (eviction) represent the single largest application category (~26,500 matters), predominantly initiated by rental providers for rent arrears (over 70% of possession claims). Following the 2021 commencement of the *Residential Tenancies Amendment Act 2018*, the tribunal applied section 330A ("reasonable and proportionate" test) in defended hearings, leading to increased payment-plan conditional adjournments, but default orders remain high when tenants fail to appear.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Court registry data tracks initiated legal processes; informal evictions, voluntary surrenders under notice pressure, and self-evictions are unobserved.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Direct Victorian legal benchmark.

### S04
- **Citation:** Productivity Commission. (2025). *Report on Government Services 2025: Part G, Section 18 (Housing) and Section 19 (Homelessness Services)*. Canberra: Commonwealth of Australia.
- **URL / Identifier:** [Productivity Commission RoGS 2025](https://www.pc.gov.au/ongoing/report-on-government-services)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Australia-wide with state breakdowns, 2023–24 data.
- **Design & Cohort:** National performance monitoring across all state/territory housing authorities and community services.
- **Key Findings:** Victoria has the lowest proportion of social housing as a percentage of total residential dwellings among all Australian states (~3.0% vs national average ~4.1%). Net recurrent expenditure per client assisted in homelessness services was $2,840 in Victoria. 12-month tenancy sustainment for public housing exceeded 94%, while tenancy sustainment following private rental brokerage was estimated at 81% (with wide confidence intervals due to survey attrition).
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Aggregated jurisdictional administrative reporting; differing intake definitions across states.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Comparative national and Victorian benchmark.

### S05
- **Citation:** Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS). (2023). *Estimating Homelessness: Census, 2021*. Cat. No. 2049.0. Canberra: ABS.
- **URL / Identifier:** [ABS Census Homelessness 2021](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/people/housing/estimating-homelessness-census/latest-release)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria & Australia, August 2021 Census data (released March 2023).
- **Design & Cohort:** Point-in-time national enumeration using statistical modelling of census returns across six homeless operational categories.
- **Key Findings:** Victoria recorded 30,660 people experiencing homelessness on Census night (47.1 persons per 10,000 population). People living in 'severely' crowded dwellings accounted for 43% of Victorian homelessness, followed by supported accommodation (28%) and boarding houses (15%). Low-income private renters living in rental stress represent the primary feeder population into marginal and homeless accommodation.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Undercount of youth, couch surfers, and people fleeing domestic violence who conceal their location; point-in-time stock measure rather than annual incidence flow.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Authoritative population baseline.

### S06
- **Citation:** Crime Statistics Agency Victoria (CSA). (2025). *Family Incidents Recorded by Victoria Police: Year to December 2024*. Melbourne: CSA.
- **URL / Identifier:** [CSA Victoria Family Violence](https://www.crimestatistics.vic.gov.au/crime-statistics/latest-victorian-crime-data)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria, calendar year 2024.
- **Design & Cohort:** Police L17 risk assessment and incident reports for all reported family violence matters statewide (98,420 recorded incidents).
- **Key Findings:** Over 98,000 family violence incidents recorded annually. Housing disruption is identified in over 35% of affected households where safety concerns mandate exclusion of a perpetrator or emergency relocation of victim-survivors and children. Financial abuse, including coerced debt and deliberate non-payment of rent, was noted in approximately 22% of high-risk cases.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Only reflects police-attended incidents; underrepresents non-physical, financial, and coercive control abuse that does not prompt a 000 call.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Primary Victorian violence and housing displacement baseline.

## Causal Effect Stratum (Trials & Quasi-Experiments)

### S07
- **Citation:** Evans, W. N., Sullivan, J. X., & Wallskog, M. (2016). The impact of homelessness prevention on homelessness. *Science*, 353(6300), 694–699.
- **DOI:** [10.1126/science.aag0833](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aag0833)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Chicago, USA; 2010–2012 cohort.
- **Design & Cohort:** Natural quasi-experiment exploiting quasi-random daily funding exhaustion at the Chicago Homelessness Prevention Call Center. Sample: 4,448 callers seeking emergency financial assistance (TFA).
- **Key Findings:** Callers who connected on days when temporary financial assistance funds were available had a **76% lower probability of entering a homeless shelter within 6 months** compared to identical callers on fund-exhausted days (0.5% vs 2.1% shelter entry rate, p < 0.01). The protective effect remained significant at 12 months (reduction of ~50%), but attenuated by 24 months. Average financial assistance was $1,040 per household. The authors calculated that the direct cost per shelter entry prevented was approximately $10,300, which compares favourably to the average public cost of a family shelter stay ($18,600).
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Low risk of bias due to plausibly exogenous funding variation; however, overall baseline shelter entry rate was low (~2%), demonstrating that most at-risk callers do not enter shelter even without assistance.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** High causal validity for short-term emergency arrears relief; transferability must account for Victoria's lower emergency shelter capacity and reliance on transitional motels.

### S08
- **Citation:** Greiner, D. J., Pattanayak, C. W., & Hennessy, J. (2013). The limits of unbundled legal assistance: A randomized study in a Massachusetts district court and prospects for the future. *Harvard Law Review*, 126(4), 901–989.
- **URL / Identifier:** [Harvard Law Review 126:901](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-126/the-limits-of-unbundled-legal-assistance/)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Massachusetts, USA; 2008–2010.
- **Design & Cohort:** Randomized controlled trial (RCT) in an eviction court comparing full legal representation (by Greater Boston Legal Services attorneys) versus limited "unbundled" legal assistance (self-help coaching and clinic advice). Sample: 129 low-income tenant households facing eviction.
- **Key Findings:** Litigants offered full representation retained physical possession of their units in **66% of cases**, compared to only **34% of cases** in the unbundled assistance group (odds ratio 4.39, p = 0.003). Tenants receiving full representation also achieved significantly larger rent waivers and procedural delays (average 8.7 months retained vs 3.2 months). Unbundled assistance alone failed to protect tenants against experienced landlord counsel.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Gold-standard RCT design; small sample size (n=129) and specific Massachusetts statutory context.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Highly informative for Victorian policy on the inadequacy of generic online legal advice (unbundled) versus funded duty lawyers at VCAT.

### S09
- **Citation:** Watson, J., Johnson, G., & Taylor, S. (2020). *Staying Home? Examining the longer-term housing outcomes of the Private Rental Assistance Program (PRAP)*. Melbourne: Unison Housing Research Lab, RMIT University.
- **URL / Identifier:** [Unison Housing Research Lab PRAP Evaluation](https://unison.org.au/research/research-reports/staying-home)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria (Western Melbourne), 2017–2019 cohort.
- **Design & Cohort:** Longitudinal prospective cohort tracking 285 low-income households assisted by Unison Housing's PRAP and PRAP Plus brokerage and case management over 12 months.
- **Key Findings:** Approximately **80% of assisted households remained housed in the private rental market at 12-month follow-up**, avoiding entry into homelessness. Single-parent families (predominantly women escaping domestic violence or experiencing sole-earner shock) represented 54% of clients and achieved 82% retention. Flexible brokerage used for rent arrears was the strongest predictor of immediate tenancy rescue; however, PRAP Plus intensive case support was required for households with multiple debts or dispute complexities.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Single-provider evaluation; observational cohort without a randomized control group (counterfactual is inferred from historical SHS entry rates); potential survivor bias in survey re-contact.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Direct primary evaluation of Victoria's flagship private rental sustainment policy instrument.

### S10
- **Citation:** Justice Connect. (2024). *Keeping Women and Children Safely Housed: 10-Year Impact and Evaluation Report of the Women's Homelessness Prevention Project (WHPP)*. Melbourne: Justice Connect Homeless Law.
- **URL / Identifier:** [WHPP 10-Year Impact Report](https://justiceconnect.org.au/resources/womens-homelessness-prevention-project-10-year-impact-report/)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria, 2014–2024 longitudinal caseload.
- **Design & Cohort:** Integrated multi-disciplinary legal and social work service evaluation across 1,120 women and children facing imminent eviction or VCAT possession proceedings.
- **Key Findings:** In finalized legal matters, **84% of women and children were successfully assisted to remain safely housed or transition to planned safe alternative housing**, directly preventing homelessness. Over 75% of clients had experienced family violence. The integrated social worker addressed secondary drivers (Centrelink suspensions, debt, mental health referrals) while lawyers defended VCAT possession applications under RTA s 330A.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Caseload-based observational study without an unrepresented control arm; clients accepted into intensive service have pre-screened legal merit.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Direct Victorian evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of combining legal advocacy with social work triage at VCAT.

### S11
- **Citation:** Gale, W. G., Gelfond, H., & Karger, E. (2022). *The impact of emergency rental assistance on eviction filings: Evidence from the pandemic ERA program*. Washington, DC: Brookings Institution / Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago.
- **URL / Identifier:** [Brookings Emergency Rental Assistance](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-impact-of-emergency-rental-assistance-on-eviction-filings/)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** United States, 2021–2022.
- **Design & Cohort:** County-level quasi-experimental panel event study assessing the US federal $46.5 billion Emergency Rental Assistance (ERA) program across thousands of municipal jurisdictions.
- **Key Findings:** The rollout of emergency rental assistance reduced municipal eviction filings by **15% to 25%** within 3 months of distribution onset. Jurisdictions with streamlined tenant-direct application processes and court diversion partnerships achieved the largest reductions, whereas jurisdictions requiring burdensome landlord verification suffered slow uptake and higher eviction rates.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Macro-administrative panel design; pandemic eviction moratoria operated concurrently, requiring complex econometric controls.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Highlights the institutional design risk: administrative friction and landlord veto power severely undermine emergency grant effectiveness.

### S12
- **Citation:** Curtis, M. A., Garboden, P. M., & Thomas, T. A. (2023). Landlord participation and tenant retention under emergency rental assistance: Quasi-experimental evidence from municipal administrative records. *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management*, 42(3), 712–739.
- **DOI:** [10.1002/pam.22485](https://doi.org/10.1002/pam.22485)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** USA (multiple urban centres), 2020–2022.
- **Design & Cohort:** Quasi-experimental analysis of 18,400 emergency assistance applications matching tenant outcomes to property registry and court records.
- **Key Findings:** Approximately 18% of eligible tenants failed to receive assistance due to landlord refusal to participate or supply tax/banking documentation. Landlords who refused were significantly more likely to own properties in gentrifying neighbourhoods and sought to evict low-income tenants to re-lease at higher market rates. Furthermore, among tenants where landlords accepted arrears payouts, 14% were served with non-renewal notices within 9 months, illustrating "landlord capture" where state funds clear past debt without securing ongoing tenure.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Focuses on US private landlord behaviour; US legal structures permit easier no-cause lease non-renewals than Victorian RTA post-2021 reforms.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Crucial warning on the limits of cash arrears assistance in the absence of statutory tenure security protections.

### S13
- **Citation:** O'Neill, M. C. (2019). *Evaluating New York City's Universal Access to Legal Services in Eviction Proceedings: First-Year Implementation and Court Dispositions*. New York: Office of Civil Justice, NYC Department of Social Services.
- **URL / Identifier:** [NYC Universal Access Report](https://www.nyc.gov/site/hra/help/legal-services-for-tenants.page)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** New York City, 2018–2019.
- **Design & Cohort:** Population-level court administrative evaluation across all five NYC boroughs tracking 25,000+ residential eviction proceedings.
- **Key Findings:** Tenants represented by counsel avoided eviction in **84% of cases** (compared to an unrepresented baseline eviction rate exceeding 50%). Residential eviction warrants issued by NYC Housing Courts dropped by 32% citywide as representation rates rose from under 10% to over 38%.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Observational administrative series; concurrent economic growth and local rent regulations contributed to declining evictions.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Demonstrates the feasibility of universal access to tenant legal representation in civil eviction tribunals.

### S14
- **Citation:** Collinson, R., & Reed, D. (2023). The effects of evictions on low-income households. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 138(4), 2185–2248.
- **DOI:** [10.1093/qje/qjad042](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjad042)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** New York & Chicago, USA; longitudinal panel 2007–2016.
- **Design & Cohort:** Instrumental variables (IV) design exploiting the random assignment of court eviction cases to lenient versus harsh judges. Sample: 100,000+ court-involved low-income tenant households.
- **Key Findings:** Winning an eviction order causes a **140% increase in emergency shelter stays** in the first year, an 18% reduction in wage earnings, a 45% increase in total unpaid collections debt, and a significant increase in emergency department admissions. The negative impacts persist for at least two years post-eviction.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Methodologically rigorous IV identification; quasi-experimental estimates reflect local average treatment effects (LATE) for marginal eviction cases.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Quantifies the counterfactual cost of non-intervention: allowing an eviction to proceed inflicts catastrophic, compounding fiscal and human costs on public health, income, and shelter systems.

## Implementation, Statutory & Economic Stratum

### S15
- **Citation:** Victorian Parliament. (1997/2021). *Residential Tenancies Act 1997 (Vic)*, incorporating amendments under the *Residential Tenancies Amendment Act 2018 (Vic)*. Melbourne: Victorian Government Printer.
- **URL / Identifier:** [Victorian Legislation RTA 1997](https://www.legislation.vic.gov.au/as-made/acts/residential-tenancies-amendment-act-2018)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria; substantive tenancy reforms commenced 29 March 2021.
- **Statutory Mechanisms:**
  - **Section 330A (Reasonable and proportionate test):** VCAT is mandated to consider whether making a possession order is reasonable and proportionate, having regard to the nature and frequency of the breach, whether the breach was trivial, whether it was caused by someone other than the tenant, whether the breach has been or can be remedied, the impact on children or vulnerable occupants, and whether any alternative order can be made.
  - **Section 91ZM (14-day notice for rent arrears & 5-strike rule):** A rental provider may issue a 14-day notice to vacate for non-payment of rent. If the tenant pays the arrears before the termination date, the notice is of no effect. However, if the tenant pays late and clears arrears 4 times in a 12-month period, on the 5th notice the rental provider may apply to VCAT for possession regardless of payment.
  - **Section 91V (Family violence lease creation):** A victim-survivor of family violence can apply to VCAT to terminate the existing lease and create a new tenancy agreement excluding the perpetrator, without liability for perpetrator-caused rent arrears or property damage.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Authoritative statutory framework governing all tenancy terminations and eviction defences in Victoria.

### S16
- **Citation:** Jacobs, K., Hulse, K., Stone, W., & Wiesel, I. (2020). *Private rental access and sustainment programs: delivery, outcomes and economic evaluation*. AHURI Final Report No. 343. Melbourne: Australian Housing and Urban Research Institute.
- **DOI:** [10.18408/ahuri5321301](https://doi.org/10.18408/ahuri5321301)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Australia (Victoria, NSW, WA); published 2020.
- **Design & Cohort:** Mixed-methods evaluation across state private rental assistance programs, combining administrative program reviews, provider interviews, and econometric cost-consequence modelling.
- **Key Findings:** Upstream private rental assistance programs cost between $1,500 and $3,500 per client episode (including brokerage and staffing). The authors demonstrate that successful sustainment generates substantial cost offsets across acute public sectors, averting an estimated $12,000 to $28,000 per sustained tenancy in downstream state expenditure (avoided crisis accommodation, reduced hospital presentations, avoided justice system interactions). However, programs are severely constrained by low private vacancy rates and real estate agent gatekeeping.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Economic models rely on literature-derived service utilization offsets rather than linked multi-agency data trials.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Core Australian policy evaluation of private rental sustainment mechanisms.

### S17
- **Citation:** Pitcher Partners. (2019). *Economic Evaluation of the Women's Homelessness Prevention Project (WHPP)*. Pro bono social impact assessment for Justice Connect. Melbourne: Pitcher Partners.
- **URL / Identifier:** [Pitcher Partners WHPP Economic Evaluation](https://justiceconnect.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/WHPP-Economic-Evaluation.pdf)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria, 2018–2019 financial year.
- **Design & Cohort:** Cost-benefit and fiscal impact assessment modelling 110 women and children assisted by WHPP in Victoria.
- **Key Findings:** Total cost per assisted client was approximately $2,100 (combining legal advocacy, social work, and flexible brokerage). By preventing eviction and entry into crisis accommodation, the project generated an estimated **$3.80 in net economic benefit for every $1.00 invested**, yielding over $1.7 million in annual avoided costs for the Victorian Government across justice, emergency housing, health, and child protection services.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Pro bono consulting report using social return on investment (SROI) framework; assumes a high counterfactual shelter entry rate (60%) for unassisted eviction cases.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Directly measures the Victorian fiscal and legal context.

### S18
- **Citation:** Victorian Auditor-General's Office (VAGO). (2020). *Managing Social Housing Allocations*. Melbourne: Victorian Government Printer.
- **URL / Identifier:** [VAGO Social Housing Allocations](https://www.audit.vic.gov.au/report/managing-social-housing-allocations)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria, parliamentary performance audit (October 2020).
- **Design & Cohort:** Comprehensive audit of Homes Victoria and DFFH allocation practices and VHR operations across all public and community housing stock.
- **Key Findings:** VAGO found that social housing allocations are heavily rationed to only the most extreme crisis cases. Long turnover times for vacant properties (averaging 30+ days for re-letting) and lack of transparent performance metrics create substantial delays. The audit established that social housing cannot function as an agile, rapid eviction-prevention mechanism under current stock levels.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Highly rigorous independent statutory audit; focus is on administrative allocation efficiency rather than private tenancy sustainment.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Authoritative proof of structural social housing supply limits.

### S19
- **Citation:** Department of Families, Fairness and Housing (DFFH). (2022). *Tenancy Plus Operational Guidelines: Supporting Social Housing Tenancies to Prevent Homelessness*. Melbourne: State of Victoria.
- **URL / Identifier:** [DFFH Tenancy Plus Guidelines](https://www.dffh.vic.gov.au/tenancy-plus-operational-guidelines)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria, operational policy active 2022–2026.
- **Design & Cohort:** Program guidelines governing 21 community agency providers delivering Tenancy Plus across Victoria for social housing tenants at risk of eviction.
- **Key Findings:** Tenancy Plus provides three tiers of support: advocacy/brokerage for emerging arrears, intensive case support for complex tenancy breakdown (mental health, hoarding, antisocial behaviour notices), and initial tenancy support for new high-risk transfers. The program achieves 88% tenancy retention during support; however, capacity is capped by agency contract funding, leading to provider waitlists and exclusion of lower-level arrears cases until formal VCAT notices are issued.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Administrative policy document; lacks independent control group evaluation.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Primary Victorian operational framework for social housing sustainment.

### S20
- **Citation:** Wood, G., Ong, R., & Cigdem, M. (2014). *Housing affordability dynamics: new insights from the HILDA survey*. AHURI Final Report No. 233. Melbourne: Australian Housing and Urban Research Institute.
- **URL / Identifier:** [AHURI Final Report No. 233](https://www.ahuri.edu.au/research/final-reports/233)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Australia, HILDA longitudinal panel 2001–2011.
- **Design & Cohort:** Econometric panel analysis tracking housing affordability stress, debt accumulation, and tenure transitions across 7,000+ Australian households over a decade.
- **Key Findings:** Low-income private renters who enter severe housing affordability stress (spending >50% of income on rent) face high exit rates into informal accommodation or homelessness unless their income increases. Crucially, the authors found that repayable debt and personal loans taken to cover rent arrears significantly compound long-term instability, locking households into poverty traps. Non-repayable grants, in contrast, provide stabilizing relief without debt overhang.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** High-quality national longitudinal econometric data; sample size for eviction-specific subgroups is relatively small.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Critical Australian empirical evidence supporting non-repayable grants over loan mechanisms.

### S21
- **Citation:** Taylor, S., & Johnson, G. (2021). Pathways through the private rental market: Longitudinal stability, affordability stress, and sustainment among low-income households. *Housing Studies*, 36(7), 1012–1034.
- **DOI:** [10.1080/02673037.2020.1744633](https://doi.org/10.1080/02673037.2020.1744633)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Melbourne, Victoria; longitudinal panel 2016–2019.
- **Design & Cohort:** Prospective longitudinal cohort study tracking 350 low-income households in Melbourne's private rental market over 24 months.
- **Key Findings:** Over 24 months, only 42% of low-income households remained in the same tenancy. Tenancy breakdown was driven primarily by rent increases (exceeding CPI/wage growth), fixed-term lease expirations, and sudden income shocks. Households receiving targeted assistance (such as PRAP) exhibited higher retention at 12 months, but by 24 months, structural rent-to-income ratios re-emerged as the dominant predictor of housing displacement.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Melbourne metropolitan focus; sample attrition over 24 months (~22%).
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Direct Victorian longitudinal evidence demonstrating the decay of short-term intervention effects over multi-year horizons.

### S22
- **Citation:** Australian National Research Organisation for Women's Safety (ANROWS). (2019). *Domestic and family violence, housing and homelessness: Research synthesis*. ANROWS Insights, Issue 04/2019. Sydney: ANROWS.
- **URL / Identifier:** [ANROWS Housing & DFV Synthesis](https://www.anrows.org.au/publication/domestic-and-family-violence-housing-and-homelessness/)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Australia-wide, 2019.
- **Design & Cohort:** Evidence synthesis reviewing 34 peer-reviewed and evaluation studies examining housing pathways for domestic and family violence (DFV) victim-survivors.
- **Key Findings:** Preserving existing tenancies ("safe at home") is highly beneficial for children's education and social stability, but carries severe risks if legal exclusion of the perpetrator is incomplete. Standard tenancy laws often hold survivors jointly and severally liable for property damage and arrears caused by the perpetrator. The synthesis highlights that tenancy sustainment must be survivor-led: forcing survivors to sustain tenancies without dedicated security upgrades and legal severance of perpetrator liability creates dangerous coerced vulnerability.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Qualitative and mixed-methods evidence synthesis; uneven jurisdictional data across Australian states.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Direct guidance on the essential intersection of RTA 1997 s 91V and family violence housing safety.

### S23
- **Citation:** Victoria Legal Aid (VLA). (2024). *Tenancy Legal Assistance and VCAT Advocacy: Service Data and Policy Submission on Eviction Prevention*. Melbourne: Victoria Legal Aid.
- **URL / Identifier:** [Victoria Legal Aid Housing](https://www.legalaid.vic.gov.au/tenancy-and-housing)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Victoria, 2023–24.
- **Design & Cohort:** Service delivery data covering 6,800 tenancy legal advices, duty lawyer appearances, and VCAT casework representations.
- **Key Findings:** Over 90% of tenants appearing unrepresented at VCAT Residential Tenancies List hearings face immediate possession orders or adverse findings. When represented by VLA duty lawyers or funded community legal centres, possession orders were avoided in 78% of defended hearings through structured repayment agreements, dismissal for defective notices, or application of RTA s 330A. VLA noted severe geographic coverage gaps, with regional VCAT venues having minimal in-person duty lawyer availability.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Legal aid administrative service records; clients receiving representation are pre-screened for eligibility and merit.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Authoritative Victorian primary frontline legal service data.

## Systematic Reviews & Methodological Critiques

### S24
- **Citation:** Shinn, M., Greer, A. L., Bainbridge, J., Kwon, J., & Winfree, S. (2013). Efficient targeting of homelessness prevention services for families. *American Journal of Public Health*, 103(S2), S324–S330.
- **DOI:** [10.2105/AJPH.2013.301468](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2013.301468)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** New York City, USA; 2010–2012.
- **Design & Cohort:** Predictive empirical screening model analyzing 11,105 families applying for municipal homelessness prevention services (HomeBase).
- **Key Findings:** The authors identify the central dilemma of homelessness prevention: the "efficiency trap." Only 10% to 15% of families seeking prevention assistance would actually enter a homeless shelter if left unassisted. Most double-up, negotiate with family, or borrow informally. Consequently, broad, un-targeted prevention programs suffer from high "deadweight loss" (assisting many families who would not have become homeless). The authors developed an empirical screening tool based on housing history, benefits, and eviction notices that increased targeting efficiency by over 300%.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** High methodological rigor; specific to NYC shelter intake administrative rules.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Essential methodological warning for Victorian programs: failing to screen for imminent formal VCAT notice risks spending limited public funds on households who would have resolved arrears informally.

### S25
- **Citation:** Burt, M. R., Pearson, C., & Montgomery, A. E. (2007). *Strategies for preventing homelessness*. Washington, DC: U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, Office of Policy Development and Research.
- **URL / Identifier:** [HUD Homelessness Prevention Strategies](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/publications/homeless/prev_homeless.html)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** United States; multi-site evaluation across 8 communities.
- **Design & Cohort:** Comprehensive national study evaluating prevention typologies: cash assistance, legal defense, housing court mediation, and landlord-tenant liaison.
- **Key Findings:** Cash assistance is highly effective for households facing a single, non-recurring income shock (e.g. temporary illness, car repair, job change), but is completely ineffective for households with chronic zero-income or ongoing structural deficits. Eviction mediation and legal aid succeed in slowing displacement and resolving disputes, but cannot prevent eviction when rent exceeds the tenant's total income capacity. The authors conclude that prevention must be strictly stratified by the nature of the crisis.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Qualitative and comparative case study synthesis; pre-dates modern algorithmic screening tools.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Strongly supports separating one-off temporary arrears assistance (PRAP) from long-term social housing subsidies.

### S26
- **Citation:** Mackie, P., Thomas, I., & Hodgson, K. (2018). Homelessness prevention: Reflecting on a new era for English and Welsh policy and legislation. *European Journal of Homelessness*, 12(3), 81–107.
- **URL / Identifier:** [European Journal of Homelessness 12(3)](https://www.feantsaresearch.org/en/european-journal-of-homelessness)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Wales & England, UK; longitudinal policy evaluation 2015–2018.
- **Design & Cohort:** Nationwide evaluation of the *Housing (Wales) Act 2014* and the English *Homelessness Reduction Act 2017*, establishing statutory duties on local authorities to take reasonable steps to prevent homelessness for anyone threatened with homelessness within 56 days.
- **Key Findings:** Statutory prevention duties increased successful tenancy sustainment from 35% to 65% of presenting households. Mediation, flexible financial relief, and private landlord negotiation were the primary mechanisms. However, the reform exposed a major vulnerability: in tight rental markets with rising private rents, local authorities increasingly struggled to prevent eviction, leading to temporary accommodation backlogs and landlord non-cooperation.

- **Applicability to Victoria:** Demonstrates the value of establishing early statutory intervention triggers (e.g. 56 days before eviction) and reveals market supply constraints.

### S27
- **Citation:** Luchenski, S., Maguire, N., Aldridge, R. W., et al. (2018). What works in inclusion health: overview of effective interventions for marginalised and excluded populations. *The Lancet*, 391(10117), 266–280.
- **DOI:** [10.1016/S0140-6736(17)31959-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(17)31959-1)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** International systematic review (UK, USA, Australia, Europe); published 2018.
- **Design & Cohort:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of 133 studies examining interventions for people experiencing extreme exclusion (homelessness, substance use, severe mental illness, justice involvement).
- **Key Findings:** Permanent supportive housing (Housing First models) achieved **80% to 90% housing retention at 12–24 months** for chronically excluded cohorts, significantly outperforming standard stepwise treatment models (relative risk of retention 1.84, 95% CI 1.45–2.33). For high-complexity cohorts, standalone cash assistance or brief legal advocacy without ongoing case coordination had negligible impact on housing stability.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** High-quality systematic review following PRISMA guidelines; focus is primarily on already-homeless cohorts rather than upstream prevention.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Demonstrates that for complex social housing tenancies facing eviction, intensive integrated support (Tenancy Plus / SSHP) is required rather than cash alone.

### S28
- **Citation:** Stone, W., Parkinson, S., Sharam, A., & Ralston, L. (2016). *Housing assistance need and provision in Australia: a new approach*. AHURI Final Report No. 262. Melbourne: Australian Housing and Urban Research Institute.
- **URL / Identifier:** [AHURI Final Report No. 262](https://www.ahuri.edu.au/research/final-reports/262)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Australia-wide, 2016.
- **Design & Cohort:** National demographic and economic modelling combining Census, HILDA, and Commonwealth Rent Assistance (CRA) administrative datasets.
- **Key Findings:** Approximately 1.3 million low-income Australian households experience housing affordability stress in the private rental market. In Victoria, over 220,000 low-income households spend >30% of their income on rent, with ~80,000 spending >50%. - **Applicability to Victoria:** Demonstrates the structural ceiling: state-level tenancy sustainment programs operate within a federal income support framework that structurally underfunds low-income renters.

### S29
- **Citation:** Parkinson, S., Batterham, D., Reynolds, M., & Mallett, S. (2019). *The changing profile of homelessness in Australia: trends, transitions and policy responses*. AHURI Final Report No. 310. Melbourne: Australian Housing and Urban Research Institute.
- **URL / Identifier:** [AHURI Final Report No. 310](https://www.ahuri.edu.au/research/final-reports/310)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Australia (Victoria, NSW, Queensland), 2011–2018.
- **Design & Cohort:** Longitudinal analysis of linked Specialist Homelessness Services National Data Collection (SHS NDC) records tracking 150,000+ client trajectories over 4 years.
- **Key Findings:** Homelessness transitions are highly dynamic: ~60% of clients who exit homelessness return to services within 24 months if placed in private rental housing without ongoing rent subsidies or living skills support. The authors identify "churn" as a primary systemic inefficiency, where households cycle repeatedly between precarious private tenancies, family doubling-up, and crisis accommodation. Tenancy sustainment interventions that provide only 3 to 6 months of relief fail to disrupt the 2-year churn cycle unless structural income or housing affordability improves.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Tracks only clients presenting to funded homelessness agencies; missing clients who exit services and secure unassisted stability or migrate.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Highlights the crucial distinction between 6-month tenancy retention and true 24-month housing stability.

### S30
- **Citation:** Desmond, M., & Gershenson, C. (2017). Who gets evicted? Assessing individual, neighborhood, and network factors. *Social Science Research*, 62, 362–377.
- **DOI:** [10.1016/j.ssresearch.2016.08.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssresearch.2016.08.017)
- **Jurisdiction & Date:** Milwaukee, USA; 2009–2011.
- **Design & Cohort:** Quantitative survey and court record matching across 2,400+ low-income tenant households.
- **Key Findings:** Eviction is not distributed randomly among poor households: **low-income women with children face dramatically higher odds of eviction than men with identical income and arrears levels** (odds ratio 2.1). Landlords perceive female-headed households with children as higher-maintenance and more disruptive. The presence of children increases eviction risk by attracting landlord scrutiny and tribunal claims for noise or damage.
- **Risk of Bias & Limitations:** Survey and court matching design in a heavily segregated US urban market; US private rental regulation allows greater landlord discretion than Victoria's RTA.
- **Applicability to Victoria:** Crucial equity evidence explaining why single-mother households represent the overwhelming majority of clients seeking Victorian tenancy sustainment (PRAP/WHPP).
